Welcome back to part two of our presentation. Now that we have explored the visual application and 3D models, let us dive deep into the technical architecture, Python code, and procedural shader algorithms that power this entire project inside Blender.

The entire solar system was programmatically generated using Blender’s native Python API, known as bpy. By writing a Python script instead of manually placing objects, the entire scene is fully automated, mathematically precise, and completely reproducible with a single command.

Our script begins with the scene initialization function called reset_scene. When executed, this function clears any default cubes, lights, or lingering datablocks by calling read_factory_settings. It configures the render engine to Blender’s real-time EEVEE engine, sets the frame rate to thirty frames per second, and establishes our timeline from frame one to frame three hundred and sixty.

Next, the script builds our cosmic environment in the create_starfield_world function. Rather than using an external skybox image, the deep space background is generated procedurally in the World shader node tree. We instantiate a Voronoi texture node with a scale of one hundred and forty. When piped through a high-contrast ColorRamp, the distance values isolate tiny, sharp pinpoints of light against a near-black background, creating a vast and twinkling field of stars that surrounds our camera from every angle.

Now, let us examine the procedural shader networks for our celestial bodies, starting with the Sun. In create_sun_material, we construct a material node tree anchored by a Principled BSDF with emission. A Noise Texture node with high detail and roughness generates organic plasma turbulence. This noise is mapped through a ColorRamp with multiple color stops: deep solar red, incandescent orange, brilliant gold, and pure white hotspots. By driving both the Base Color and Emission Color with this ramp and setting Emission Strength to fourteen, the Sun produces a vibrant, self-illuminating aura. We complement this with an eighteen-thousand-watt Point Light placed at the origin, ensuring that the light actually illuminates other planets and casts dynamic shadows across the scene.

Next, let us look at the most sophisticated shader in the project: the Earth material. To achieve a realistic look without downloading image textures, we developed a multi-layered procedural recipe in create_earth_material. We use a high-detail Noise Texture to generate continental landmasses. A finely tuned ColorRamp translates noise values into geographical biomes: deep navy for ocean trenches, lighter cyan for shallow coastlines, sandy beaches, lush green vegetation, and brown mountain highlands. To create the polar ice caps, we take the Object coordinates, isolate the vertical axis using a Separate XYZ node, and apply an Absolute math node. This treats the North and South poles symmetrically, painting pure white snow caps above a specific latitude. We also derive the surface roughness from the landmass noise, setting water to a mirror-like roughness of zero point zero eight so it reflects sunlight, while continents remain rough and matte at zero point eight five. A Bump node provides physical displacement relief for mountain ranges, and a Layer Weight Fresnel node creates a subtle blue rim glow that mimics Earth’s atmospheric horizon.

Hovering just above Earth is our secondary cloud sphere. Its material, create_earth_cloud_material, uses an Alpha Blend mode where a soft noise texture controls the alpha transparency, allowing the continents below to be seen through clear skies while fluffy cloud formations cast real-time shadows.

For Jupiter, the create_jupiter_material function combines a Wave Texture set to Bands along the Z-axis with noise distortion, recreating Jupiter's iconic swirling atmospheric jet streams. For Saturn’s rings, we calculate the radial distance from the planet's center using Pythagoras theorem in shader math: the square root of X squared plus Y squared. We feed this into a Map Range node and a multi-stop ColorRamp that defines the transparent inner edge, the dense B-ring, the dark Cassini Division gap, and the outer A-ring.

Finally, let us discuss how the animation is engineered. Rather than baking thousands of static keyframes, we implement Blender Drivers on the rotation_euler attributes. By attaching a driver expression like frame times speed to the Z-rotation of each planet’s orbital pivot, the planets continuously revolve around the Sun forever without keyframe interpolation artifacts. Mercury moves at speed zero point zero four, Earth at zero point zero one eight, and distant Neptune at zero point zero zero two two, perfectly mirroring Kepler's third law of planetary motion.

This clean combination of procedural shaders, mathematical drivers, and structured collection management showcases the immense power of code-driven 3D animation in Blender. Thank you for watching!
